01 / THE QUESTION

What can income patterns tell us about neighborhood change?

This project examines Bayview–Hunters Point through household income data and research on gentrification.

The aim is to connect changes in income distribution to the broader discussion of neighborhood change. A statistical pattern is a starting point for asking about a place, its residents, and the pressures they may face.

How are household income patterns changing, and how should we interpret those changes in a neighborhood context?

02 / THE APPROACH

From geographic boundaries
to a research narrative.

The work brings together a defined study area, household income tables, and research on gentrification and displacement.

  1. 01

    Define the place.

    Use San Francisco neighborhood definitions to frame the study area, while considering how neighborhood boundaries relate to Census geography.

  2. 02

    Organize the evidence.

    Work with American Community Survey household income tables to examine the distribution of households across income brackets and years.

  3. 03

    Connect numbers to context.

    Bring the income patterns into conversation with urban research on gentrification, displacement, and neighborhood change.

03 / READING THE DATA

A neighborhood is more
than a table.

Interpreting income data requires attention to the geography, time period, and limits of the evidence. The following questions frame how I present this research.

Are the places comparable?

Neighborhood names and Census tracts describe different geographies. Changes in tract boundaries can also affect comparisons across years.

Do the income brackets mean the same thing over time?

Dollar values in different ACS releases reflect different years' purchasing power. Comparisons need to account for that distinction.

What can income data tell us about displacement?

A change in an area's income distribution does not, by itself, establish who moved, why they moved, or whether a household was displaced.
